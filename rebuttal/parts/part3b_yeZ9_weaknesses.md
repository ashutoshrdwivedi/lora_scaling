**W1 (uniform rank constraint).** Addressed under Q5: pad-to-max is latency-neutral because Finding 3 measures its exact worst case, the true cost is per-adapter memory, and per-rank stores with rank-bucketed batching serve mixed-rank fleets today with zero padding and no kernel changes. The same argument covers uniform target modules — an adapter omitting a module contributes a zero delta there, bounded by the same delta-path share (1.56% of a projection's arithmetic at $r{=}8$), and removed entirely by bucketing or by the masked-BMM variant we flag as future work.

**W2 (single GPU / single node).** Partially addressed, partially conceded. **New:** hardware generality — the full sweep replicated on an L40S-48GB, an inference-tier Ada card with GDDR6 rather than HBM, showing the same $O(1)$-in-$N$ behaviour (p50 at the 28,000 ceiling within +1.27% of its $N{=}1000$ value) and 4.1–32.4× over PEFT-mixed. **Conceded:** this is hardware generality, *not* multi-node scaling, and does not address the concern raised. Multi-GPU serving remains architectural (Appendix C). Because adapter pools shard by tenant with no cross-GPU communication we expect replication to scale near-linearly, but we have not measured it, and we will label those claims as design rather than results — not letting the new hardware row imply more than it shows.

**W3 (no empirical custom-kernel comparison).** The bound is **measured, not analytic**, and we should have made that clearer. Finding 6 rests on three quantities and the binding one is a direct ablation: we run the identical batch with the entire LoRA path disabled — gather, shrink/expand and merge — and measure the wall-clock difference, **2.4 ms of 26.4 ms (9.0%)**. A custom fused kernel touches only that path, so 9.0% is a hard ceiling on what any kernel could recover; it cannot beat deleting the work. The other two legs are the analytic FLOP ratio $3d/r = 384$, bounding free-kernel FLOP savings at 0.26%, and a profiler trace charging 4.94% of forward GPU time to the LoRA `bmm` against 50.6% to the base projections. Note the 9.0% is a wall-clock share, not a FLOP share — the gather and merge, not the `bmm`, account for the gap.

The new configurations tighten this rather than leaving it untested: $3d/r$ grows with width — 384 at $d{=}1024$, 576 at $d{=}1536$, 960 at $d{=}2560$ — so the maximum recoverable FLOP share falls from 0.26% to 0.17% to 0.10%. The case for custom kernels gets *weaker* as encoders get larger, the opposite of the decoder-side trend that motivates them. A direct comparison remains impossible because, to our knowledge, no custom-kernel implementation serves encoder LoRA — Punica, S-LoRA and LoRAX target decoder architectures, and vLLM's LoRA-pooling path supports only decoder-backbone embedders. That absence is the paper's motivating gap; we state it as unavailability, not as evidence of our own optimality. Porting a BGMV-style kernel to encoders to verify the bound is future work.

**W4 ($N{=}8$ few-shot accuracy only).** Conceded. We follow the SetFit protocol to keep the accuracy study comparable to prior art, and whether LoRA configuration choices matter more in full-data regimes is a fair open question. We would note the scope this affects: the serving claims — latency, throughput, tenant ceiling, cold-start — are independent of adapter *quality*, since the tensor shapes are fixed by $(L, d, r)$ and the arithmetic is identical whether the weights were trained on 8 examples or 8,000. The accuracy table establishes that late fusion is numerically faithful, reproducing per-tenant LoRA outputs to $<10^{-4}$ relative against PEFT on real checkpoints, not that a particular training budget suffices. We will make that division explicit.

**W5 (CPU bottleneck at high QPS).** Now characterized experimentally — see Q1/Q3. On a lightweight encoder the baseline assembler caps single-stream throughput at 6,804 samples/s with assembly at 50–58% of latency, and the measured `index_select` assembler lifts this to 15,342 samples/s in pure PyTorch while collapsing assembly share to 1.8–4.1% and the p99/p50 tail ratio to 1.01×. We will move this from Limitations into the evaluation at camera-ready.

**W6 (embedding/reranking workloads not directly evaluated).** bge-m3, the model we serve throughout, *is* a retrieval/embedding encoder, so the benchmarked forward — shared base, per-tenant delta, pooled output — is the same compute an embedding workload executes; the newly added XLM-R-XL is from the same family. And the serving path is head-agnostic: the batched per-tenant head is one output choice, and returning the pooled embedding instead removes work rather than adding it. A cross-encoder reranker is the same encoder forward over a query-document pair. We concede we did not run an end-to-end retrieval or reranking evaluation under load, and will scope the wording to "encoder serving with per-tenant heads", naming reranking explicitly as untested.
